A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. An ion has the same protons but a different number of electrons, so it carries a charge. Ions form when electrons move. The nucleus doesn’t change.

Core vocabulary

anion
A negatively charged ion formed by gaining electrons. It is attracted to cations.
cation
A positively charged ion formed by losing electrons. It is attracted to anions.
chemical formula
A notation that shows which elements are in a compound and how many atoms (or ions) of each there are.
chemical nomenclature
A universally agreed-upon set of rules for naming and classifying chemicals.
electronegativity
How strongly an atom attracts electrons toward itself when it bonds with another atom.
formula unit
The lowest whole-number ratio of ions in an ionic compound, like one Na+ for every one Cl− in NaCl.
intramolecular force
A force that holds atoms together in a compound. Another name for a chemical bond.
ion
An atom or molecule with a charge because it gained or lost one or more electrons.
ionic bond
A chemical bond formed by the electrostatic attraction between positively and negatively charged ions.
ionic charge
An ion’s positive or negative charge, caused by the loss or gain of electrons from an atom or group of atoms.
ionic compound
A substance made of positive and negative ions held together by ionic bonds.
ionization energy
The minimum energy needed to remove one valence electron from a neutral atom or molecule.
IUPAC rules
The standard set of rules, from the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, used to name chemical compounds.
Lewis dot structure
A diagram that shows an element’s valence electrons as dots.
main group metals
Metals in Groups 1 and 2 (the s block). They form cations with only one possible charge.
metals
Elements that generally have low ionization energies and tend to lose electrons to form cations.
multivalent metal
A metal that can form more than one charge, so its name needs a Roman numeral.
nonmetals
Elements that typically have high electronegativity and tend to gain electrons to form anions.
octet rule
An atom tends to gain, lose, or share electrons to end up with eight electrons in its outermost shell.
oxidation number
A number that describes an atom’s charge by how many electrons it has gained or lost.
polyatomic ion
A group of covalently bonded atoms that acts as a single unit with an overall charge.
Roman numerals
The numerals in parentheses after a multivalent metal’s name. They show the metal’s charge.
subscripts
The small numbers after a symbol that show how many atoms (or ions) of that element are present. No number means one.
transition metals
Elements in Groups 3–12. Most can form more than one ion, and they typically have a partially filled d orbital.
valence electron
An electron in the outermost shell of an atom. These are the electrons that take part in bonding.
Memory tricks

Cations have a cat in them, and cats have paws, so cations are PAWS-itive. There’s also a plus sign hiding in the middle of ca+ion. Anion = A Negative ION.

Why atoms bond

Most elements don’t stay on their own. They bond with other elements to lower their energy and reach a more stable arrangement of electrons.

A compound is two or more elements chemically bonded, with no overall charge. The bonds are made with valence electrons. Compounds are sorted into two main types, ionic or covalent, based on the kind of bond the elements make. To decide which type you have, look at what kinds of elements are involved and compare their electronegativities.

How an ionic bond forms

The octet rule says atoms give away or take electrons until their outer shell holds eight. (Hydrogen is the exception. It aims for two electrons in its first shell, or none at all if it loses its electron.)

Metals have low ionization energy, so they lose electrons easily and become cations. Nonmetals have high electronegativity, so they pull electrons in and become anions. When a nonmetal’s strong pull meets a metal’s weak grip, one or more electrons transfer. The resulting oppositely charged ions attract each other by electrostatic attraction and lock into a regular, repeating crystal pattern.

Watch out

The ionic bond isn’t the electron jumping from one atom to another. The transfer creates the ions. The bond is the electrostatic attraction between those ions.

Predict the bond type with electronegativity

Subtract the two electronegativity values (bigger minus smaller). The size of the difference tells you what kind of bond to expect.

Electronegativity differenceBond type
Less than 0.4Nonpolar covalent
0.4 to 1.7Polar covalent
Greater than 1.7Ionic

Example: sodium is 0.93 and chlorine is 3.16. The difference is 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23. That’s greater than 1.7, so the bond is ionic. Sodium transfers an electron to chlorine, making a cation and an anion held together by electrostatic attraction.

Watch out

Bonds aren’t simply “ionic” or “not ionic.” Bond character is a continuum, and no bond is ever 100% ionic. The bigger the electronegativity difference, the more ionic the bond is. Also, books draw the cutoff lines in slightly different places, so use the values your teacher gives you.

Draw ionic compounds with Lewis structures

A Lewis structure shows why a formula is written the way it is. You draw the electron transfer first, then show the finished ions.

  1. Identify the ions. Find the metal (it will lose electrons and become a cation) and the nonmetal (it will gain electrons and become an anion). Write the metal on the left and the nonmetal on the right.
  2. Count valence electrons. Use each element’s group number. Add that many dots around the symbol.
  3. Show the transfer. Draw arrows from the metal to the nonmetal. Add more atoms until the metal has lost all its valence electrons and every nonmetal has a full octet.
  4. Finalize. Rewrite each ion in brackets with its charge. Use subscripts when you need more than one ion. Check that the charges add up to zero.

Properties of ionic compounds

The type of bond in a substance affects how it behaves. Ionic compounds share a set of properties, and every one of them traces back to ions held in place by electrostatic attraction.

PropertyWhat you’ll observeWhy
Crystal structureHard crystals with a regular shapeIons pack into a repeating pattern called a crystal lattice, which adds to the compound’s stability and strength.
High melting and boiling pointsNeeds a lot of heat to meltThe strong electrostatic forces between ions take a large amount of energy to overcome.
BrittleShatters when hit instead of bendingA hit shifts the lattice so ions of the same charge line up and repel each other.
Dissolves in waterMany (not all) ionic compounds dissolveWater molecules are polar. They surround and separate the individual ions.
Conducts electricityNot as a solid. Yes when dissolved in water (or melted).Ions in a solid are locked in place. Dissolved ions are free to move and carry charge.

What makes an ionic bond stronger?

The attraction is stronger when the charges on the ions are larger and when the ions are smaller, because small ions can get physically closer together.

Watch out

Charge isn’t the only factor. Ion size and electronegativity difference matter too.

Writing ionic formulas

A chemical formula uses element symbols to show which elements are in a compound and subscripts to show how many of each. A symbol with no subscript means one.

Ionic compounds don’t form separate molecules. Huge numbers of ions pack together into a crystal. So an ionic formula shows a formula unit, the lowest whole-number ratio of ions. For sodium chloride that’s one sodium ion for every one chloride ion, so the formula is NaCl. The positive and negative charges balance, so there’s no overall charge.

Watch out: charge is not the subscript

Ionic charges are written as superscripts (Mg2+) and show how many electrons an atom gained or lost. Subscripts show how many of each ion are in the formula unit (MgCl2). Don’t mix them up. Your Lewis structures can help you find the charges and how many of each ion you need.

Four guidelines for ionic formulas

  1. The net charge must be zero.
  2. The cation is always listed before the anion.
  3. The formula of a polyatomic ion is written as a unit.
  4. If more than one polyatomic ion is needed, put it in parentheses with a subscript after.

Balance the charges

Take Mg2+ and Cl−. One magnesium ion has a charge of +2. Each chloride ion is −1, so it takes two of them to cancel: MgCl2.

The cross-and-drop shortcut gets the same result:

  1. Write the ions with their charges: Al3+ and O2−.
  2. Cross the charge numbers. The 3 becomes the subscript on O. The 2 becomes the subscript on Al. Drop the signs.
  3. Reduce to the lowest whole-number ratio if you can.
  4. Check: 2 × (+3) = +6 and 3 × (−2) = −6. Net charge is zero. The formula is Al2O3, the compound in your rubies and sapphires.
Don’t skip the reducing step

Sn4+ and O2− cross to Sn2O4, but the lowest ratio is SnO2. Also keep practicing the “balance the charges” way of thinking, not just the shortcut. It’s the one that still works when a metal can have more than one charge.

Naming ionic compounds

Naming follows IUPAC rules, a standard system that keeps scientists everywhere from getting confused. In every ionic name and formula, the cation comes first and the anion comes second.

Binary ionic compounds

Binary means two elements: one metal ion and one nonmetal ion. When the metal has a fixed charge, say the metal’s name, then the nonmetal’s name with the ending changed to -ide. MgCl2 is magnesium chloride. Mg3N2 is magnesium nitride.

NonmetalIon nameCharge
Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine (Group 17)fluoride, chloride, bromide, iodide1−
Oxygen, sulfur (Group 16)oxide, sulfide2−
Nitrogen, phosphorus (Group 15)nitride, phosphide3−
Remember

Ionic names don’t use number prefixes. MgCl2 is “magnesium chloride,” not “magnesium dichloride.” The ratio comes from the charges. Say the anion names out loud while you look at the periodic table. It helps you remember the spelling and sound of the “-ide” endings.

Compounds with polyatomic ions

A polyatomic ion is a group of covalently bonded atoms that acts as one unit with an overall charge. Naming is the same as for binary compounds: cation name, then anion name. Ca3(PO4)2 is calcium phosphate. Keep the polyatomic ion’s name as it is. Don’t change its ending to “-ide.”

Watch out

Not every polyatomic ion needs parentheses. Use them only when you need more than one of that ion. NaOH has none. Ca(OH)2 needs them.

Metals that can have more than one charge

Some metals form more than one kind of ion. These are multivalent metals. Most transition metals are multivalent, and so are tin and lead. These metals need an extra step in the name: write the metal’s name, then its charge as a Roman numeral in parentheses, then the anion.

MetalPossible charges
Iron2+, 3+
Copper1+, 2+
Tin2+, 4+
Lead2+, 4+
Chromium2+, 3+ (and 6+ in some compounds)
Watch out

Roman numerals aren’t just for transition metals. Tin and lead need them too. But Groups 1 and 2, aluminum, and the transition metals zinc, cadmium, and silver form only one ion, so they don’t get a Roman numeral.

Why does the charge matter? Iron and sulfate can make two different compounds: FeSO4 is iron(II) sulfate, and Fe2(SO4)3 is iron(III) sulfate. “Iron sulfate” alone wouldn’t tell you which one you have.

Writing a formula from a name: tin(IV) bromide means Sn4+ with Br−. It takes four bromide ions to balance one Sn4+, so the formula is SnBr4.

Finding the charge from a formula: in Fe2(SO4)3, three sulfate ions are 3 × (−2) = −6. The two iron ions must total +6, so each is 3+. The name is iron(III) sulfate.

Watch out

The subscript on the anion isn’t always the metal’s charge. In MnO2, the two O2− ions total 4−, so one Mn is 4+, not 2+. Ionic formulas are always written in the smallest ratio of ions.

Naming, step by step

  1. Find the cation. Is it a metal, or polyatomic like NH4+?
  2. If it’s a metal, decide whether it needs a Roman numeral. Balance the charges to find its charge.
  3. Find the anion. A single nonmetal ion ends in -ide. A polyatomic ion keeps its name.
  4. Write the cation name (with the Roman numeral if needed), then the anion name.
